Binary Forecast · 30-Day Window

Iran–U.S. War, 23 April – 23 May 2026

By Unmitigated Wisdom  ·  23 April 2026

Two separate yes/no questions for the next thirty days of the Iran–U.S. crisis — will hot war resume, and will a permanent peace deal be announced. Working from an accuracy-weighted blend of six expert lenses and current evidence, the answer to both is no, but with very different confidence.

Headline Forecasts

Q1. Will hot war resume within the next month? — No. 66% no / 34% yes. 80% subjective interval for “yes”: 23%–46%.

Q2. Will a permanent peace deal be publicly announced or signed by both governments within the next month? — No. 91% no / 9% yes. 80% subjective interval for “yes”: 4%–17%.

These are two separate marginal forecasts, not a three-way partition. A brief resumption of hot war and a later peace announcement within the same 30-day window is logically possible, though I assess that joint path as low probability.

The simplest mistake to make in this crisis is to mistake an indefinite ceasefire for a settlement, and a coercive maritime standoff for active war. The current pause is neither. Iran has not formally accepted Trump's open-ended ceasefire extension; the Strait of Hormuz remains effectively shut; the U.S. blockade is in force; Israel is openly waiting for permission to resume strikes. That is the world this 30-day forecast is operating in.

Definitions

Both questions need narrow definitions to be useful.

“Hot war resumes” does not mean the current background level of blockade enforcement, ship seizures, mine-clearing, or maritime harassment — those are already happening. I define hot-war resumption as a return to sustained strategic air and missile strikes, major interstate naval combat, or comparably intense direct U.S./Iran or Israel/Iran military action above the present coercive-maritime baseline.

“Permanent peace deal” is defined narrowly: a public announcement or signed agreement by both governments presented as a lasting end to hostilities. A vague ceasefire extension, indirect mediation, or a temporary technical understanding does not qualify.

Methodology

The forecast uses a two-layer method. First, the Expert Prediction Accuracy Tracker[11] serves as a structured prior on which analysts have historically been strongest on which kinds of questions. The tracker covers 64 experts, 501 predictions, and 461 resolved cases, scored on a TRUE / MOSTLY TRUE / PARTIALLY TRUE / MOSTLY FALSE / FALSE scale, with unresolved calls excluded; included predictions had to be falsifiable, forward-looking, specific, and attributable. I treat that dataset as a curated prior, not as ground truth.

Second, I do not mechanically average those scores. I weight each expert mainly inside their strongest domain:

  • Bob McNally — energy markets, Hormuz, oil/LNG disruption.
  • Jennifer Kavanagh — U.S. strategic incentives, coercion vs. time horizons.
  • Wes Rumbaugh — missile-defense inventories, cost-exchange ratios, readiness constraints.
  • Hossein Bastani — Iranian regime structure, succession, IRGC institutional depth.
  • Danny Citrinowicz — Tehran's behaviour under pressure, deterrence logic, Gulf leverage.
  • John Mearsheimer — structural incentives, escalation logic, limits of military coercion.

The current strategic picture

The present condition is not peace.

Reuters reported on 22 April that Trump said he was extending the ceasefire indefinitely, but Iranian officials did not clearly confirm agreement, and Tehran stated that a full ceasefire only made sense if the U.S. blockade was lifted. The same reporting noted that the Strait of Hormuz remained effectively shut, that Iran had seized two ships, and that vessel traffic had collapsed from roughly 130 per day pre-war to just a handful.[1] On 23 April, Reuters further reported that there was no formal extension and no announced plan for additional talks.[2]

The military friction is rising rather than fading. Reuters reported that Israel said it was waiting for a U.S. “green light” to resume attacks, while Iran said it would not consider reopening Hormuz until Washington lifted the blockade.[2] AP reported the same day that Trump ordered the U.S. military to “shoot and kill” Iranian small boats laying mines and to intensify mine-clearing efforts.[3] Brookings described the talks as “in limbo” and warned that an indefinite ceasefire still allows Iran to impose major economic damage.[4]

This is best understood as an armed coercive pause, not a peace process.

The six expert lenses

Bob McNally — the energy-and-Hormuz lens

McNally's signature line is: “The market is overly optimistic about resumption of Strait of Hormuz.” That captures his strongest pattern: he is best when the question is not simply political intent, but how coercion translates into flows, spare capacity, LNG chokepoints, and market stress.

His tracker profile shows strong performance on U.S. strike probability, Hormuz disruption, and LNG vulnerability, with weaker results on some medium-term market-structure calls. For this forecast, McNally pushes hard against any quick-peace thesis. Bloomberg reported on 17 April that he believed any improvement in Hormuz would reverse without a real breakthrough, and that even with a deal it could take three to four months for supply traffic to normalise.[5]

Jennifer Kavanagh — the strategic-mismatch lens

Kavanagh's key line is: “The Houthis are undeterred.” Her broader pattern: she is strongest at identifying situations where Washington is using force in ways that are tactically active but strategically misaligned.

Her current argument is especially important. On 22 April she argued that the blockade is “unlikely to work” because of a “mismatch of stakes and time horizons”: Iran sees the conflict as existential and can endure pain longer than Trump, who wants a rapid political result.[6] This logic points away from both quick capitulation and quick peace.

Wes Rumbaugh — the constraint-and-inventory lens

Rumbaugh's analytical signature is captured by the phrase “No THAADs 'til 2027.” He is most useful not as a forecaster of Tehran's intentions, but as an analyst of the cost structure and inventory arithmetic behind U.S. decisions.

His strongest calls have been on missile-defense depletion and bad cost-exchange ratios between cheap attacking systems and expensive interceptors. His importance to the current forecast is indirect but real: renewed high-intensity war is not impossible, but it becomes strategically less attractive when it burns scarce missile-defense inventories at a faster rate than they can be replenished.

Hossein Bastani — the regime-structure lens

A Bastani-style core proposition can be summarised as: Iran–U.S. relations are not normalising, and the regime should be analysed as an institutional system, not a headline event.

His strongest historical performance is on IRGC institutional depth, succession, and the overestimation of overthrow scenarios. His most important contribution here is that the regime appears brittle but not broken. That lowers the expected payoff from renewed bombing, because the political effect is unlikely to match the military effort.

Danny Citrinowicz — the Iran-behaviour-under-pressure lens

Citrinowicz's key line is: “Iran's leadership operates under its own sunk-cost logic.” Among the six experts he has the highest tracker score and is the single strongest guide to Iranian bargaining behaviour under pressure.

In his 15–17 April material his position sharpens into three claims:

  1. Iran does not want endless war, but it will not surrender under pressure.
  2. Hormuz is now a usable coercive lever, not merely a threat.
  3. Any arrangement without credible enforcement and technical precision will be unstable.

His current public framing is even more concise: “You cannot beat geography,” and in any renewed round, “closing the strait will be the first thing in the Iranian textbook.”[7]

John Mearsheimer — the structural-escalation lens

Mearsheimer's key line is: “He has no good military option at this point.” His main value here is not personality reading but structural logic: what happens when coercion lacks a plausible path to political closure.

On 22 April he wrote that Trump lacks a “viable military option” against Iran and therefore has strong reasons to extend the ceasefire and pursue negotiations instead of reopening major war.[8]

Synthesis

The Convergent Proposition

Iran can continue imposing serious costs without reopening full-scale war, and the United States can continue imposing pressure without solving the problem.

  • McNally explains the leverage mechanism: Hormuz remains the hinge.
  • Kavanagh explains why blockade pressure alone is strategically mismatched.
  • Rumbaugh explains why another intense round would be expensive in readiness terms.
  • Bastani explains why the regime can absorb punishment without collapsing.
  • Citrinowicz explains why Tehran will bargain from defiance, not surrender.
  • Mearsheimer explains why Washington lacks a military path that obviously turns escalation into political closure.

That synthesis strongly supports a continued coercive standoff as the modal near-term condition.

Binary 1 — Will hot war resume in the next month?

Forecast: No. 66% no / 34% yes.

The strongest case for no is structural. The current equilibrium is costly, but it is still more attractive to both sides than immediate major escalation:

  • Washington wants pressure without another large strategic round if it can avoid it.
  • Tehran wants leverage without inviting maximal destruction.
  • Israel is willing to resume attacks, but Reuters reports it is still waiting for a U.S. “green light.”[2]

Kavanagh's argument about the “mismatch of stakes and time horizons” is central here.[6] Mearsheimer's argument that Trump lacks a “viable military option” reinforces it.[8] Reuters' March reporting that U.S. intelligence did not see imminent regime collapse also matters, because it lowers the expected political value of renewed bombing.[9] The same case is reinforced by the elevation of Mojtaba Khamenei with decisive Revolutionary Guard backing.[10]

So why is the “yes” probability still as high as 34%? Because the current equilibrium is highly fragile. AP's report on the new mine-clearing rules of engagement substantially increases the chance that a tactical clash turns into something larger.[3] Reuters' reporting that Israel is actively prepared for renewed attacks keeps the veto-player risk alive.[2] Brookings is right to say the situation remains ripe for return to an active military phase if talks do not resume.[4]

Triggers that would flip this forecast

A fatal maritime clash; a successful Iranian mine operation causing major casualties; a major strike on neutral or third-country shipping; or an Israeli decision that waiting is worse than acting. Each is plausible enough on its own that 34% remains the right yes-probability rather than something lower.

The most likely path is a continuation of blockade, seizures, mine-clearing, threats, and coercive bargaining without a return to sustained strategic bombing.

Binary 2 — Will a permanent peace deal be announced or signed in the next month?

Forecast: No. 91% no / 9% yes.

The immediate reason is simple: diplomacy is not yet on a timeline consistent with a formal permanent bilateral peace announcement within 30 days. Reuters reported on 23 April that there was no formal ceasefire extension and no announced plan for further talks.[2] Tehran's position, as reported by Reuters, is that Hormuz will not be reopened unless the blockade is lifted.[2] Brookings says peace talks are “in limbo.”[4] Reuters also reported that Trump said he did not want to be rushed into a deal and preferred a lasting arrangement over a quick one.[1]

The substantive gap is also large. Reuters reported that Washington wants Iran to give up highly enriched uranium and further enrichment, while Iran wants sanctions relief, compensation, and recognition of its position over the strait; the same reporting says Tehran has tied truce talks to Hezbollah-Lebanon issues.[1] Even if a temporary understanding is possible, a publicly announced permanent end-of-hostilities agreement by both governments is a much narrower and harder event.

The expert synthesis supports the same conclusion. McNally implies that even after a deal, normalisation would take months.[5] Kavanagh implies coercion alone will not produce fast capitulation.[6] Bastani and Citrinowicz both imply Tehran is bargaining from hardline resilience, not weakness. Mearsheimer implies Trump wants negotiations, but not that an actual permanent settlement is close.[8]

The most plausible alternative to renewed hot war is not peace, but an extended dirty truce.
