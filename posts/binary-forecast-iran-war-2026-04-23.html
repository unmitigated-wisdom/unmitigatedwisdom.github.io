Binary Forecast · 30-Day Window

Iran–U.S. War, 23 April – 23 May 2026

By Unmitigated Wisdom  ·  23 April 2026

Two separate yes/no questions for the next thirty days of the Iran–U.S. crisis — will hot war resume, and will a permanent peace deal be announced. Working from an accuracy-weighted blend of six expert lenses and current evidence, the answer to both is no, but with very different confidence.

Headline Forecasts

Q1. Will hot war resume within the next month? — No. 66% no / 34% yes. 80% subjective interval for “yes”: 23%–46%.

Q2. Will a permanent peace deal be publicly announced or signed by both governments within the next month? — No. 91% no / 9% yes. 80% subjective interval for “yes”: 4%–17%.

These are two separate marginal forecasts, not a three-way partition. A brief resumption of hot war and a later peace announcement within the same 30-day window is logically possible, though I assess that joint path as low probability.

The simplest mistake to make in this crisis is to mistake an indefinite ceasefire for a settlement, and a coercive maritime standoff for active war. The current pause is neither. Iran has not formally accepted Trump's open-ended ceasefire extension; the Strait of Hormuz remains effectively shut; the U.S. blockade is in force; Israel is openly waiting for permission to resume strikes. That is the world this 30-day forecast is operating in.

Definitions

Both questions need narrow definitions to be useful.

“Hot war resumes” does not mean the current background level of blockade enforcement, ship seizures, mine-clearing, or maritime harassment. Those are already happening. I define hot-war resumption as a return to sustained strategic air and missile strikes, major interstate naval combat, or comparably intense direct U.S./Iran or Israel/Iran military action above the current coercive-maritime baseline.

“Permanent peace deal” is defined narrowly: a public announcement or signed agreement by both governments that is presented as a lasting end to hostilities. A murky ceasefire extension, indirect mediation, or a temporary technical understanding does not count.

Methodology

The forecast uses a two-layer method. First, the Expert Prediction Accuracy Tracker serves as a structured prior on which analysts have historically been strong on which types of questions. The tracker covers 64 experts, 501 predictions, and 461 resolved cases, scored on a TRUE / MOSTLY TRUE / PARTIALLY TRUE / MOSTLY FALSE / FALSE scale, with unresolved predictions excluded; included calls had to be falsifiable, forward-looking, specific, and attributable. I treat that dataset as a curated prior, not as ground truth.

Second, I do not mechanically average those scores. I weight each expert mainly inside their strongest domain — McNally on energy and Hormuz, Kavanagh and Rumbaugh on U.S. strategic constraints and stockpiles, Bastani and Citrinowicz on regime structure and Tehran's behaviour under pressure, and Mearsheimer on structural escalation logic. The current strategic picture

The present situation is not peace. Reuters reported on 22 April that Trump said he was extending the ceasefire indefinitely, but Iranian officials did not say they had agreed, and Tehran said a full ceasefire only made sense if the U.S. blockade was lifted. The same reporting noted that the Strait of Hormuz remained effectively shut, that Iran had seized two ships, and that vessel traffic had collapsed from roughly 130 per day pre-war to just a handful. On 23 April, Reuters reported there was no formal extension of the ceasefire and no plans announced for further talks.

Military friction is rising, not fading. Reuters reported on 23 April that Israel said it was waiting for a U.S. “green light” to resume attacks, while Iran said it would not consider reopening Hormuz until Washington lifted the blockade. AP reported the same day that Trump ordered the U.S. military to “shoot and kill” Iranian small boats laying mines and to intensify mine-clearing efforts. Brookings described the diplomatic picture as being “in limbo” and warned that an indefinite ceasefire still allows Iran to do “massive damage to the global economy.”

Binary 1 — Will hot war resume in the next month?

The answer is probably not, but the risk is material.

The strongest case for no resumption is structural. Jennifer Kavanagh argues that the blockade is “unlikely to work” because of a “mismatch of stakes and time horizons”: Iran is treating the conflict as existential and can afford patience, while Trump wants a fast, decisive result that blockade pressure cannot deliver. John Mearsheimer's current view points in the same direction — he says Trump lacks a “viable military option” and has a strong incentive to stop the economic damage. Reuters' March reporting also matters here: U.S. intelligence assessed that Iran's government was not at imminent risk of collapse, and Reuters separately reported that Mojtaba Khamenei was elevated with decisive Revolutionary Guard backing, reinforcing the view that renewed bombing would be costly without guaranteeing political closure.

Together, these six lenses point to an ugly but intelligible equilibrium: Iran can keep imposing pain without reopening a full strategic campaign, and Washington has reasons to avoid another all-out round unless forced by events.

The reason I do not push the “no hot war” probability higher is that the current equilibrium is fragile. AP's report about the shoot-and-kill order sharply raises the odds of a tactical encounter spiraling upward. Reuters says Israel is openly preparing for a restart if Washington agrees. Brookings says that if the parties do not get back to the table, the conflict is “still very ripe” and may return to an “active military phase.” The main danger is not that one side has found a clean path to victory; it is that coercive maritime friction and veto players make accidental or semi-deliberate escalation easy.

Triggers that would flip this forecast

A fatal maritime clash; a successful mine-laying operation that kills Americans or major third-country crews; an Israeli decision that waiting is worse than acting. Each is plausible enough on its own that 34% remains the right yes-probability rather than something lower.

Binary 2 — Will a permanent peace deal be announced or signed in the next month?

The answer is almost certainly not.

The immediate reason is simple: the diplomacy is not yet on a track that plausibly ends in a formal bilateral peace announcement within 30 days. Reuters reported on 23 April that there was no formal extension of the ceasefire and no plans announced for further talks. The same reporting says Tehran will not consider opening Hormuz until the United States lifts the blockade, and that Iran is demanding the blockade end before it resumes serious movement. Brookings says peace talks are “in limbo.” Reuters also reported on 23 April that Trump said he does not want to be rushed into a deal and prefers a lasting arrangement over a quick fix. None of those are the conditions under which a permanent bilateral peace agreement normally appears quickly.

Substantively, the gap remains too wide. Reuters says Washington wants Iran to give up highly enriched uranium and further enrichment, while Iran wants sanctions relief, reparations, and recognition of its control over the strait; Reuters also reports that Iran has tied truce talks to Hezbollah-Lebanon issues. Brookings' 23 April discussion says the sequencing problem is still unresolved and the divide between the parties remains considerable. In plain terms: even if a temporary arrangement or another ceasefire understanding is possible, a publicly announced permanent end to hostilities signed off by both governments is a much narrower and harder event.

Here again the expert synthesis points the same way. McNally implies that even with a deal, supply normalization would take months, not days. Kavanagh's model says coercion alone will not generate a fast capitulation. Rumbaugh's framework says Washington has reasons to avoid renewed war, but not that it has found a stable settlement formula. Bastani and Citrinowicz both point to a regime that is hardened, Guard-influenced, and structurally capable of bargaining from defiance rather than surrender. Mearsheimer says Trump wants negotiations, but the road is full of obstacles. The Citrinowicz posts of 15–17 April fit the same pattern: Iran may negotiate, but from a position that treats Hormuz as leverage and treats pressure alone as inadequate to force ideological concessions.

The only realistic way this forecast fails is if Washington suddenly redesigns the blockade into a negotiating off-ramp, Tehran reciprocates quickly, and Israel accepts a near-term settlement framework rather than pressing for another round. Current evidence does not make that the base case.

The revised binary forecast is straightforward.

The logic tying both forecasts together is this: the current pause is most consistent with a dirty coercive standoff, not with real settlement and not yet with deliberate full-scale resumption. That middle condition is unstable, which is why hot-war risk is still substantial; but it is still more likely than either an immediate relapse into all-out fighting or a clean, formal peace accord.
